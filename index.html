The Violinist Who Went to War

Mordechai's Story

The night before I entered Gaza, I played violin for my unit. Thirty years of music had taught me how to carry people through moments too heavy for language.

Then the music stopped. I put the violin down, picked up my gear, and went in.

I fought in Gaza as a combat soldier and came home with injuries that changed the shape of my life. But surviving the battlefield was only half the fight — the other half was surviving the rehabilitation system. Endless forms, committees, appeals. Most wounded veterans don't have the strength for it. Many give up.

I'm an engineer. I build things. So I built Magen — an AI platform that guides wounded veterans through every step of their rehabilitation, in plain human language, fighting the bureaucracy alongside them. I built it from my truck — my home on wheels — the same truck I've traveled in for years, giving hundreds of lectures and violin performances across Israel and the world.

A combat veteran living in a truck. A violinist who writes software. A man of faith who builds AI. None of it was planned. All of it is real.

When you adopt a wounded soldier, you're telling someone: your sacrifice matters. You are not forgotten. As someone who has played violin for soldiers on the worst night of their lives — I know how much it means when someone simply shows up.

A note about Magen

I'm grateful for your willingness to support me — it means more than I can say. But if I'm honest, there are soldiers out there who need it more than I do.

That's why I built Magen. Every conversation the AI has with a wounded veteran — every form it helps fill out, every appeal it drafts — costs money to run. Right now, that cost is what keeps Magen from reaching everyone who needs it.

So here's what I'd love: let's do this together. Help me keep Magen running — so it can reach the friends and brothers who are still fighting the system alone.
